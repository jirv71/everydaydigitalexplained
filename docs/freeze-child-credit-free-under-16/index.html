How Do I Freeze My Child's Credit for Free? (Under 16)

Last verified 2026-10-30 against 7 official sources

Short answer: If your child is under 16, the FTC says you can request a free credit freeze at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. The FTC says a freeze makes it harder for someone to open new accounts in your child's name, and it stays in place until you ask the bureaus to remove it. The process is different from an adult freeze. Each bureau asks for copies of documents that prove who you are, who your child is, and that you're the parent or guardian, and all three describe sending them by mail. Most children don't have a credit file, so the bureau creates one just to freeze it.

  • Send copies, not original birth certificates or Social Security cards. Experian says it doesn't return what you send.
  • A child freeze is free. If a site asks you to pay or start a subscription, you're looking at a different product, not the free freeze.
  • Use the mailing addresses on each bureau's own minor freeze page. Don't use addresses or links from unexpected emails, texts, or calls.

Step by step

  1. Gather copies of your documents. The FTC lists your driver's license or other government ID, proof of your address (like a utility bill or an insurance statement), your child's birth certificate, and your child's Social Security card. If you aren't the parent, you'll also need papers showing you're the legal guardian. Send copies, not originals.
  2. Freeze at Equifax. Equifax says parents and legal guardians must mail the request. Fill out its Minor Freeze form and mail it with copies of your documents to Equifax Information Services LLC, P.O. Box 105788, Atlanta, GA 30348. Equifax says it may take up to 3 business days after it gets everything, because it usually has to create a file first.
  3. Freeze at Experian. Use the "Add or remove a security freeze for a minor" form at experian.com/help/minor-request, print it, and mail it with copies of your documents to Experian, PO Box 9554, Allen, TX 75013. Experian says most people hear back within 10 to 15 days. Questions: 1-888-397-3742.
  4. Freeze at TransUnion. Mail a written request to place a "Protected Consumer Freeze" on your child's file, a copy of proof you can act for your child (such as a birth certificate), and proof of your identity and your child's. TransUnion's address for these freezes is TransUnion, P.O. Box 380, Woodlyn, PA 19094. It says it can't take these requests by phone or online.
  5. Keep the confirmation letters. Equifax says it sends a confirmation letter once the freeze is placed, or a letter asking for more documents. Keep every letter with your child's important papers. You'll need the same kind of proof later to lift or remove the freeze.
  6. If a credit report turns up, report it. The FTC says a child under 18 generally won't have a credit report. If a bureau finds one, contact the fraud department of each company where an account was opened, ask the bureaus to remove fraudulent accounts, and report it to the FTC at IdentityTheft.gov.

Who can freeze a child's credit

The FTC and CFPB say a federal law that took effect September 21, 2018 lets parents, legal guardians, and child welfare representatives request a free security freeze for someone under 16. It's also free to unfreeze.

For a child in foster care, the FTC says a child welfare or probation agency representative can ask for the freeze. They must show papers saying the child is in the agency's care, such as an official letter from the agency.

Teens who are 16 or 17 can request and remove a freeze themselves, the FTC says. Equifax says they can do it by phone or by mail.

Why the bureau may create a file for your child

Most children don't have a credit file. The FTC and CFPB say that if a bureau has no file on your child, it will create one so it can freeze it, and that record can't be used for credit. Experian and Equifax say the same thing on their own pages.

TransUnion says a credit file usually starts when someone applies for credit, opens an account, or a public record appears. Adding a child as an authorized user on someone's card can also start one.

What each bureau asks for

Equifax: one ID for you (a driver's license or other government ID, your Social Security card, or your birth certificate); one paper showing you can act for the child (the child's birth certificate, a court order, a valid power of attorney, or a foster care certification); and both the child's Social Security card and birth certificate.

Experian: the printed form, your driver's license or other government ID, proof of your address (such as a bank statement, utility bill, or insurance statement), your child's birth certificate, your child's Social Security card, and proof of guardianship if you aren't named on the birth certificate. Experian says it doesn't return what you send, so mail copies only.

TransUnion: a written request for a "Protected Consumer Freeze," a copy of one paper proving your authority (such as a court order, a valid power of attorney, a current guardianship document, or a government document showing parentage, including a birth certificate), and proof of identity for you and your child, such as a Social Security number or card, a certified birth certificate, or a driver's license or other government ID.

How to check if your child already has a credit report

The FTC suggests asking each of the three bureaus for a manual search of your child's Social Security number. It uses the same documents listed above.

Experian says parents of children 13 or younger must write to it to ask, and it usually answers within 10 to 15 days. TransUnion has an online Child Identity Theft Inquiry Form on its credit freeze FAQ page. TransUnion recommends checking with the other bureaus too, because what each one has can be different.

The FTC lists signs to watch for: calls about an overdue bill for an account you didn't open, government benefits denied because someone is already using your child's Social Security number, an IRS letter about your child's unpaid taxes, or a student loan denied because of bad credit.

Common questions

Is it free to freeze my child's credit?

Yes. The FTC says if your child is under 16 you can request a free credit freeze, and the FTC and CFPB say it's also free to unfreeze. You do have to contact each of the three bureaus separately.

Can I freeze my child's credit online?

Usually not. Equifax and TransUnion say parents must send minor freeze requests by mail, and TransUnion says it can't take them by phone or online. Experian's minor freeze form is also printed and mailed.

My child doesn't have a credit report. Can I still freeze it?

Yes. The FTC and CFPB say that if a bureau has no file on your child, it will create one so it can freeze it, and that record can't be used for credit.

What happens to the freeze when my child turns 16?

It stays. Equifax says the freeze stays in place until your child decides otherwise. The FTC says teens who are 16 or 17 can request and remove a freeze themselves.

Does a freeze stop all child identity theft?

No. The FTC says a freeze makes it harder for someone to open new accounts in your child's name. Someone could still misuse a Social Security number in other ways, such as for taxes or government benefits, so watch for the warning signs the FTC lists.
