What does payroll software do when you hire your first employee?

Last verified 2026-10-21 against 16 official sources

Short answer: Full-service payroll software figures each paycheck, withholds and pays payroll taxes, and files the forms; Gusto, for example, says it files W-2s and reports new hires for you. But the IRS says you, the employer, are still ultimately responsible for federal payroll tax deposits and payments, even when a payroll company does the work. You also still decide if your helper is really an employee, get an EIN, collect Form W-4, complete Form I-9 and check that deposits were made. For 2026 wages, W-2s are due to employees and the Social Security Administration by February 1, 2027.

Step by step

  1. Decide whether your helper is an employee or a contractor. Under IRS common-law rules, a worker is generally your employee if you have the right to control what will be done and how it will be done. The IRS looks at three kinds of facts: behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. If you're not sure, you or the worker can file Form SS-8 to ask the IRS to decide.
  2. Get an EIN and your state accounts. Apply for an Employer Identification Number at IRS.gov/EIN. It's free, and the IRS warns to beware of websites that charge for one. The SBA says to find out whether you need state or local tax IDs, and you may need to register with your state workforce agency for unemployment insurance.
  3. Do the day-one paperwork. Ask your new employee to fill out Form W-4 for federal income tax withholding. The employee must complete Section 1 of Form I-9 by their first day of work, and you must look at their documents and complete Section 2 within 3 business days of the hire date. Keep Form I-9 in your files; don't send it to USCIS.
  4. Report the new hire to your state. The IRS says you must report every new employee to your state's new-hire registry. The SBA's example reports within 20 days of the hire date; check your state's deadline.
  5. Withhold and pay payroll taxes. From each paycheck, withhold federal income tax based on the employee's W-4, plus 6.2% Social Security tax and 1.45% Medicare tax. You pay a matching 6.2% and 1.45%. For 2026, Social Security tax stops at $184,500 of wages; Medicare has no limit. You also owe federal unemployment (FUTA) tax: 6.0% of the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, or 0.6% if you get the full credit for paying state unemployment tax.
  6. Deposit on time and file each quarter. In its first calendar year, a new employer that files Form 941 deposits on a monthly schedule. That means each month's payroll taxes are due by the 15th of the next month. Deposits must be made electronically, for example through EFTPS, which is free. File Form 941 by April 30, July 31, October 31 and January 31, unless the IRS tells you in writing to file Form 944 once a year, and file Form 940 for FUTA once a year.
  7. Send W-2s and keep your records. For 2026 wages, give each employee a Form W-2 and file Copy A with the Social Security Administration by February 1, 2027. The usual January 31 date moves to the next business day because January 31, 2027 is a Sunday. Keep all employment tax records for at least 4 years.

Employee or contractor: the IRS common-law rules

The IRS says what matters is whether you have the right to control the details of how the work is done, even if you give the worker freedom. Publication 15-A says a worker is an independent contractor if you have the right to control or direct only the result of the work, not the means and methods of getting it done.

The IRS groups the facts into three categories. Behavioral: does the business control, or have the right to control, what the worker does and how they do the job? Financial: does the business control the business side of the job, such as how the worker is paid and whether they have unreimbursed expenses? Type of relationship: are there written contracts or employee-type benefits, and is the work a key part of your regular business?

If you treat an employee as a contractor and have no reasonable basis for doing so, the IRS says you may be held liable for employment taxes for that worker. The Labor Department uses a different test, called the "economic reality" test, for federal minimum wage and overtime law. It says that test is broader than the common-law standard. It proposed new rules on the test on February 26, 2026.

What payroll software automates

A full-service payroll service takes your employees' pay rates and hours, figures each paycheck and the taxes to withhold, and pays your team. Gusto, for example, says on its website that all local, state and federal payroll taxes are calculated, filed and paid automatically, and that it issues and files W-2s and 1099s and sends them to employees and contractors. It lists Forms 941 and 940 among the federal forms it files.

Gusto also says it automatically reports new hires to the government, lets new employees e-sign forms like the W-4 and I-9 when they onboard themselves, and can run payroll automatically each pay period. Other payroll services publish their own feature lists, so compare exactly what each one files and pays for you.

The IRS says you can have a payroll service or other trusted third party make your electronic federal tax deposits. Those services may charge a fee; EFTPS, IRS Direct Pay and your IRS business tax account are free.

What stays your responsibility

The IRS says the employer is ultimately responsible for depositing and paying federal tax liabilities. If a payroll company doesn't pay, the IRS may charge penalties and interest to your account. The IRS suggests three habits. Register for EFTPS with your own PIN and check your payments regularly. Keep your own address on file with the IRS, not the provider's. And treat a provider's first missed or late payment as a red flag.

Software also can't decide these things for you: whether a worker is an employee or a contractor; looking at the employee's documents for Form I-9; entering correct hours and pay rates; and paying at least minimum wage and any overtime owed. Under federal wage law, the Labor Department says to keep payroll records for at least 3 years, and the time cards and other records that wages are figured from for 2 years.

State rules are yours to check, too. The SBA says unemployment insurance varies by state, and it lists workers' compensation as a required benefit, provided through a commercial carrier, on a self-insured basis or through a state program. If your state hasn't repaid money it borrowed from the federal government for unemployment benefits, it's a "credit reduction state," and you pay extra FUTA tax.

Common questions

Do I need payroll software for just one employee?

It's your choice. The SBA lists "choose an in-house or external service for administering payroll" as one of the steps of hiring, and IRS Publication 15 explains how to figure, deposit and report the taxes yourself. If you do it yourself, you use the withholding steps in Publication 15-T and make deposits electronically, for example through EFTPS.

If my payroll company makes a mistake, who is responsible?

You are. The IRS says the employer is ultimately responsible for the deposit and payment of federal tax liabilities, and it may charge penalties and interest to your account if a third party doesn't pay. That's why the IRS suggests checking your deposits with your own EFTPS login.

When are W-2s due for 2026 wages?

By February 1, 2027, both to your employees and to the Social Security Administration. That's because January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday. Even if you get an extension to file, you still must give employees their W-2s by February 1, 2027.

Do I have to pay FUTA tax for one helper?

Generally yes, if in 2025 or 2026 you paid wages of $1,500 or more in any calendar quarter, or had at least one employee for some part of a day in 20 or more different weeks. For 2026, FUTA is 6.0% of the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, or 0.6% if you get the full 5.4% credit for state unemployment tax. You deposit it once the amount you owe is over $500.

Can I just pay my helper as a contractor to keep it simple?

Only if they really are an independent contractor under the rules. If you treat an employee as a contractor without a reasonable basis, the IRS says you may be held liable for the employment taxes. If you're unsure, file Form SS-8 or talk to a tax professional.
